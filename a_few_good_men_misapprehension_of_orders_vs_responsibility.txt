Would following orders absolve a person of responsibility for the outcome of those orders?

No — and A Few Good Men is built around why that misapprehension persists.

The misapprehension is this: responsibility = obedience.

If that were true, Dawson and Downey would be innocent, Jessup would be blameless for giving the order, and Kaffee would be blameless for taking a plea. Everyone in the chain would have done their job, and Santiago would still be dead with no one responsible.

The film argues for a different definition: responsibility = obedience to lawful orders + judgment about the order itself.

Think of it in three misapprehensions:

1. Jessup — Goal confused with method

Jessup's stated goal is to protect the nation. His misapprehension is that any method that serves that goal is therefore responsible.

He orders a Code Red to "train" Santiago and maintain discipline. In his mind, protecting the unit justifies breaking the rule the unit exists to uphold.

But a commander's responsibility is not to protect the institution at any cost. It is to protect the mission by lawful means. When the method violates the law, it does not serve the goal — it corrupts it.

Jessup's famous defense — "You can't handle the truth!" — is not an argument. It is a confession that he has replaced responsibility with necessity.

2. Dawson and Downey — Obedience confused with honor

Dawson and Downey's stated goal is to be good Marines. Their misapprehension is that a good Marine never questions an order.

They carry out the Code Red because they were told to. They believe loyalty means doing what they are told, even when they sense it is wrong.

But honor is not obedience. Obedience is a tool of honor, not a substitute for it. A soldier's responsibility includes refusing an unlawful order, because the oath is to the law and the Constitution, not to a single commander.

Dawson's final realization is the moral center of the film: he did not dishonor himself by disobeying Jessup. He dishonored himself by obeying him.

3. Kaffee — Winning confused with defending

Kaffee's initial goal is to win efficiently — take a plea, keep his record, avoid trial. His misapprehension is that a lawyer's responsibility is to get the best outcome with the least risk.

Galloway forces the correction: a defense lawyer's responsibility is zealous defense of the client, even when that means risking a loss.

Once Kaffee stops optimizing for his own record and starts optimizing for the truth of what happened to Santiago, he stops being a plea-bargain manager and starts being a lawyer.

The trap in the question is the implied reversal: "If I was ordered to do it, I did not choose to do it."

That does not follow. Following an order is still a choice. It is a choice to outsource judgment to someone else.

That outsourcing is sometimes necessary — an army cannot function if every order is debated. But it is never absolute. When an order is clearly unlawful, the responsibility returns to the person carrying it out.

If responsibility disappeared every time an order was given, no one would ever be responsible for anything done in a hierarchy. The top would blame the bottom for carrying it out. The bottom would blame the top for ordering it. The system would produce outcomes with no author.

A Few Good Men refuses that loop. Someone is always responsible, even — especially — inside a chain of command.
