In A Few Good Men, if the command had investigated first, found no faults, and had not announced it, would it make any sense for them to approach in order to announce that the investigation had uncovered no wrongs?

No — and in that story, it would make less than no sense. It would be self-defeating.

The misapprehension is this: announcing a clean result clears you.

In a hierarchy engaged in a cover-up, announcing a clean result incriminates you. It does three things, all of them harmful to the command:

1. It creates the investigation it was trying to deny

Until the announcement, there is no public investigation. There is only a dead Marine, Pvt. Santiago, and two Marines, Dawson and Downey, charged with his murder.

The second the command announces, "We investigated and found nothing," it has told everyone:

- There was something worth investigating
- They investigated it themselves
- They decided the outcome

In a court-martial context, that is not exoneration. That is a confession that they knew there was a problem to look into.

An unannounced investigation that finds no faults can stay unannounced because no expectation was created. An announced no-fault finding creates the expectation retroactively.

2. It destroys the cover story they were actually using

The command's real strategy in the film is not to announce a clean investigation. It is to announce a dirty prosecution.

Prosecute Dawson and Downey for murder. Make them the story. Make Santiago the weak Marine who could not cut it. Close the case.

That works precisely because there is no announced investigation of Jessup or of the command.

The moment Jessup announces, "We looked into ourselves," Kaffee gets to ask the obvious follow-up in court:

"Who ordered the investigation? What did you look at? Who did you interview? Where are the notes? Why did you investigate a routine death at all if there was nothing wrong?"

You cannot announce a no-fault finding without also announcing the suspicion that justified the investigation.

3. It inverts the burden in exactly the way Jessup fears

Jessup's whole philosophy is: the wall holds because you do not question the people on the wall.

A public "we found nothing" announcement invites questioning. It says, "Ask us about this."

That is the last thing a command engaged in a cover-up wants. Silence lets them control the narrative: two low-level Marines acted alone. An announcement gives the defense a thread to pull.

That is why, in the movie, the real investigation has to be forced into the open by Kaffee and Galloway. It does not come voluntarily from the command. It comes through cross-examination, subpoenas, and finally Jessup losing his temper on the stand.

The trap in the question is the implied reversal: "If we announce we found nothing, people will believe there was nothing to find."

That does not follow. In an adversarial setting, a voluntary, self-conducted, self-cleared investigation is not evidence of innocence. It is evidence of awareness.

Innocent commands do not preemptively clear themselves. Guilty ones do, because they need to get ahead of the story before someone else tells it.

If responsibility disappeared every time a command announced it had investigated itself, no command would ever be responsible for anything. The top would investigate the bottom, the bottom would be blamed for carrying out the order, and the top would clear itself for giving it.

A Few Good Men refuses that loop. The announcement that would have cleared Jessup is precisely the announcement that would have convicted him.
